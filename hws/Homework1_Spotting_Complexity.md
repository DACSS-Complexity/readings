# Homework 1 — Spotting Complexity

**Based on:** Weaver (1948), Simon (1991), Simon (1973), Kollman & Page (2006)

## The task

For each of the five passages below, classify the system being described as one of:

- **Simplicity** (Weaver)
- **Disorganized complexity** (Weaver)
- **Organized complexity — without reflexivity** (Weaver's biological-style case)
- **Organized complexity — with reflexivity** (Weaver's social/economic-style case)

For each one, write **1–2 sentences** justifying your classification. A good justification names *which test* did the work — how many variables are involved, whether they're organically interrelated, and whether the actors involved observe an aggregate (or each other) and change behavior because of it. You do not need to write more than that — a precise short answer beats a long one.

There's no single "trick" to watch for in every passage, but at least one of these five is not what it first appears to be. Read carefully before you classify.

## Passage 1

Spacecraft sent to Mars don't fly in a straight line — they follow a curved path called a Hohmann transfer orbit, first worked out by the German engineer who designed it as the most fuel-efficient route between two orbits. The entire trajectory is governed by Kepler's laws of planetary motion and Newton's law of gravitation: given the spacecraft's launch velocity, its position, and the gravitational pull of the bodies involved, engineers can calculate the exact path and travel time before the rocket ever leaves the ground.

*(Source: adapted from NASA and general orbital-mechanics references, 2025–2026)*

## Passage 2

Life insurance companies price policies using mortality tables — statistical charts built from historical death records across an entire population, broken down by age and sex. A mortality table cannot tell you when any specific policyholder will die; actuaries themselves describe these tables as "fundamental statistical tools" for measuring the probability of death across a large group, not a way to predict an individual's exact lifespan. What the table *can* do reliably is tell an insurer, across thousands of policyholders, roughly how many will die this year — reliably enough to price a profitable product.

*(Source: adapted from Asia Pacific Actuarial Conference and Social Security Administration actuarial publications, 2025)*

## Passage 3

In April 2025, global stock markets fell sharply over the course of about a week after the U.S. announced a sweeping new round of tariffs. Analysts covering the episode point to a familiar pattern: as prices started dropping, some investors sold to cut their losses, which pushed prices down further, which triggered more selling from investors watching the same falling prices — a dynamic commonly called "herd behavior" in financial market research. One review of past crashes notes that this pattern also drove the bursting of the dot-com bubble: individual investors kept buying based on rising prices, until large institutional investors started selling, at which point individual investors "quickly change their mind," triggering a broader sell-off.

*(Source: adapted from Wikipedia's "2025 stock market crash" entry and The Conversation, "Why stock markets crash," 2025–2026)*

## Passage 4

Roughly 84% of the world's coral reefs have been affected by an ongoing global bleaching event, driven by rising ocean temperatures. Coral reefs are built by many interacting species — calcifying coral animals, the algae living symbiotically inside them, and the fish and other organisms that depend on the reef structure — and researchers studying the crisis increasingly analyze reefs at the level of genetic diversity, tracking which coral populations carry heat-tolerant gene variants likely to survive continued warming. Individual coral polyps do not alter their behavior based on how the reef elsewhere is faring; each colony's fate is governed by its own thermal tolerance and local conditions.

*(Source: adapted from the "2023–2025 global coral bleaching event" Wikipedia entry and Nature Communications, 2025)*

## Passage 5

Real-time navigation apps like Waze and Google Maps route drivers around congestion by pulling in live traffic data and rerouting users onto faster paths — often quiet residential streets never designed for high traffic volumes. Transportation researchers describe this as "induced demand": because the apps are optimizing each driver's individual trip, not managing the road network as a whole, a shortcut that saves one driver time becomes congested itself once enough other drivers are routed onto it the same way — at which point the app reroutes everyone again. One widely cited case: in 2017, Google Maps, Waze, and Apple Maps rerouted drivers from a congested New Jersey highway onto the narrow streets of the small town of Leonia, creating a jam so severe that residents couldn't get out of their own driveways.

*(Source: adapted from AlgorithmWatch, IEEE Spectrum, and "Human-AI Coevolution" (arXiv, 2023), reporting on Leonia, NJ and similar cases)*
