# Session 01 — Understanding Complexity
### Video script (mind map walkthrough, ~6–8 min)

## Opening (0:00–0:45)

## Q1: What can be considered complexity? (0:45–2:30)

Our first question: what actually counts as a "complex" problem? Weaver, writing in 1948, says science has really only learned to handle two kinds of problems well.

[Zoom to Q1 branch]

The first is **simplicity** — problems with just two, three, four variables, where you hold everything else constant. That's classical physics: a ball rolling, a pendulum swinging. Enormously successful, but only because the problem is small.

The second is **disorganized complexity** — problems with millions of variables, where each one individually is erratic or unpredictable, but the average behavior of the whole system is orderly. Think statistical mechanics, or an insurance company: they can't predict when any one person will die, but they can predict very reliably how many will, on average, this year.

Here's the twist. Weaver says there's a huge middle region science barely touches: **organized complexity**. Many variables — but not millions, and not just averaged out — instead, interrelated with each other as a coherent whole. Not chaos, not simplicity. Organized.

[Point to "Answer" node]

And his punchline is: most of what we care about in biology, economics, and social life — lives right there, in organized complexity. That's the unsolved region.

---

## Q2: Weaver says complexity shows up wherever "organization" exists — so what IS organization? (2:30–4:30)

Here's the problem. Weaver uses the word "organization" as the defining feature of the hardest kind of complexity — but he never actually tells us what organization *is*. He names it and moves on.

[Zoom to Q2 branch]

This is where Simon comes in — specifically his 1991 paper on organizational learning. Simon's answer: **an organization is a system of roles.**

But — and this is the part students usually get wrong — a role is *not* a fixed set of behaviors. Simon is very explicit about this. A role is a set of **decision premises**: it tells you where to look for information, what goals count as legitimate, and what techniques to use when you're reasoning through a problem. Two people in the same role could behave very differently and still be filling that role correctly.

So why do organizations exist at all? Simon's answer connects straight back to something you'll hear a lot in this course: **bounded rationality**. No single human mind can hold an entire complex problem in its head at once. Organizations are how humans divide a problem that's too big for any one person to solve alone.

[Point to "Answer" node]

Organization, then, is the social structure we build specifically to cope with problems too large for one mind.

---

## Q3: Are organizations natural, then? (4:30–6:00)

If organizations exist because minds are limited — are they natural? Something that just emerges, like a flock of birds?

[Zoom to Q3 branch]

No. Simon's answer, across both papers, is that organizations are **built** — deliberately designed, and just as deliberately redesigned. In the 1991 paper, he gives a great example: U.S. Steel didn't just drift into becoming an investment conglomerate — its whole role structure had to be reinvented, retrained, and in many cases, staffed by entirely new people, because the *representation* of what the company was for had changed.

Now bring in the second Simon paper, from 1973. Simon argues that **technology is doing the same kind of work**. Computers and information systems are also built — deliberately designed tools — to help organizations manage decisions and, crucially, manage *attention*, which Simon says is actually the scarcer resource in an information-rich world, not information itself.

[Point to "Answer" node]

So the answer to Q3: organizations and technology are both **artificial** — human-made tools for coping with complexity we can't grasp directly. Neither one is natural. Both are built responses to the same underlying problem.

---

## Where this is going (6:00–7:00)

[Zoom to final branch]

There's a deeper version of this argument — the "artificial versus natural" distinction — that Simon develops fully in a later book, *The Sciences of the Artificial*. That's not part of this week's readings, so I won't unpack it here — ask me if you're curious.

But here's the thread I want you to leave with today: **agent-based modeling is the next tool in this same lineage.** It's an explicit, buildable, computational way of representing organized complexity — the very thing Weaver told us science hadn't yet learned to handle.

Next session, we're going to get much more precise about what "complexity" actually means in a social system — giving the concept sharper boundaries instead of the broad brush we used today.
